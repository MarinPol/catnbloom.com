Cottagecore arrived as a recognisable trend around 2020, but the sensibility it describes is much older. At its core it is a longing for slowness — for gardens, handcraft, natural materials, and a pace of life that feels sustainable rather than overwhelming.

## Where it comes from

The visual language draws from several traditions at once: English pastoral painting, Victorian botanical illustration, Scandinavian folk art, and the quieter corners of the Arts and Crafts movement. What makes cottagecore distinct from simple nostalgia is its deliberate quality. People choose it. It is an aesthetic position, not just a memory.

## Why it keeps returning

Every few years a version of this sensibility resurfaces under a different name — hygge, slow living, dark academia, goblincore. Each iteration shares something with the others: a resistance to the dominant speed of contemporary life, and a preference for textures and objects that carry a sense of history.

Cats appear in this aesthetic naturally. They are creatures of habit and comfort. They choose warmth, softness, and stillness. They belong in botanical interiors.

## What this means for design

For illustrated design, cottagecore offers a stable visual vocabulary — florals, mushrooms, herbs, soft light, earthy palettes — that connects immediately with a wide audience. It is not trend-chasing. It is tapping into something that a significant number of people already feel.

The challenge is to bring something specific to it. To find the detail that makes a design feel observed rather than assembled from references.

That is what the Cottagecore Cats collection attempts: illustrations that feel like they came from somewhere particular, not from a mood board.
