The T-shirt started as Navy underwear in 1913. Over the following decades, it moved out from under the uniform and became a canvas for pictures, jokes, slogans and all kinds of personal expression.

We draw cats and flowers for T-shirts, so at some point we wanted to know where this odd little canvas came from. This note is what we found. Every date below was checked against museum, archive or newspaper sources, and the list is at the end.

## It started as underwear

In 1913, the U.S. Navy's uniform regulations added a light, short-sleeved, buttonless undershirt for sailors. It was meant to stay hidden under the uniform.

In 1938, Sears began advertising a cotton tee as both an undershirt and an outershirt. That's one line in a catalog, but it's an early moment when a store told customers it was fine to be seen in one.

## Hollywood noticed

Marlon Brando wore one in *A Streetcar Named Desire* in 1951. James Dean followed in *Rebel Without a Cause* in 1955. After that, a plain white tee stopped looking like underwear and started looking like attitude.

Then, in 1960, Jean Seberg walked the Champs-Élysées in *Breathless*, selling newspapers in a shirt printed with the New York Herald Tribune name. A shirt with words on it, worn on purpose, on screen. That's already a graphic tee.

## Printing caught up

Printed shirts existed earlier. A green tee printed with "OZ" shows up in *The Wizard of Oz* in 1939, and in 1942 a printed Air Corps Gunnery School tee made the cover of *Life*.

But the 1960s made printing practical at scale. Around that time, Michael Vasilantone developed a rotary screen-printing machine that made multicolor shirt printing faster and more practical. It helped graphic T-shirts move into wider production, and pictures, not just letters, became realistic to print in quantity.

## Flowers and protest

"Flower power" was coined in 1965, when the poet Allen Ginsberg suggested that anti-war protesters hand out flowers instead of shouting. By the summer of 1967, Haight-Ashbury was full of flowers, bright colors and tie-dye.

We'll admit we're biased here. Flowers on clothing are not a recent mood. People have been wearing them on purpose for a long time.

On May 1, 1970, a group of lesbian activists walked onto the stage of the Second Congress to Unite Women in New York wearing shirts that read "Lavender Menace." They had dyed the shirts lavender in a bathtub. The name was a joke aimed at an insult, and the shirt turned it into a banner.

## 1977: a logo and a cat

Two very different pictures landed on shirts in 1977.

Milton Glaser designed the I ♥ NY logo in 1976, sketching it in a taxi, and the tourism campaign it belonged to launched in 1977. It went on to be printed on more shirts than anyone could count.

The same year, Crazy Shirts in Hawaii got the rights to print B. Kliban's cats. The company sold them exclusively for more than two decades. According to the Honolulu Advertiser, Kliban Cat merchandise once made up half of the company's sales.

We find that oddly comforting. People will happily wear a drawn cat.

## Slogans go mainstream

Designer Katharine Hamnett launched slogan T-shirts in 1983. In March 1984, she went to a Downing Street reception for London Fashion Week and met Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher wearing a shirt that read "58% Don't Want Pershing." It was a reference to polls about American nuclear missiles being stationed in Europe. She has said she put the slogan on the shirt at the last minute.

The photo of the two of them is still one of the best-known images in fashion. A T-shirt had become a place where a person could say something out loud.

## Where our shirts fit

Look at the list again. Navy shirts carried nothing. Seberg's carried a newspaper. The Lavender Menace shirts carried a name. Kliban's carried a cat.

Each time, someone put something they cared about where other people could see it. That's the whole trick, and it hasn't changed.

A shirt is a strange canvas. It's soft, it creases, and nobody ever sees it lying flat. That's something we think about every time we place a cat or a bouquet on one.

Our designs are cats, flowers and small stories, drawn by us and printed and shipped by TeePublic and Redbubble. For a small studio, print-on-demand means we don't need a big print run to put a drawing on a shirt.

Trends come and go, but people keep making room for things they love: cats, flowers, a bit of humor, a certain mood. We're glad to add our own drawings to that long habit.
